\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

The AUSP protocol is a communication protocol that I created for my Bachelor
thesis and later submitted to the IEEE IoT Magazine under the title
\emph{Design and Experimental Validation of a Low-Cost Acoustic Communication
Protocol for Underground Sensor Networks}. The goal of the protocol is to let
two devices talk to each other using only very cheap hardware, namely a speaker
and a microphone. The protocol sends packets by emitting pairs of superimposed
tones: at every step one tone identifies the logical channel (the carrier) and a
second tone carries the data symbol.

On the receiver side an FFT moves the microphone signal from the time domain to
the frequency domain, so that the pair of active frequencies can be read out as
peaks in the spectrum. To keep the detection reliable in the presence of noise I
had implemented, in the original thesis, several techniques: a machine learning
stage that learns the noise floor and the low frequency roll-off of the cheap
microphone (electret capsules lose sensitivity at low frequency), and a parabolic
interpolation that refines the position of each spectral peak to better than one
bin.

\subsection{What this version is}

The version documented here is a full custom FPGA implementation of the
\emph{master} side of my protocol, paired with an ESP32 in a hybrid arrangement.
The FPGA is a Tang Nano 20K (Gowin GW2AR-18). I used Gowin EDA for synthesis,
place and route and programming, and GHDL to simulate my VHDL before putting it
on the board.

On the FPGA I preferred to write the interconnect, the peripherals and the memory
controller myself in VHDL, and to drive their registers from a soft core CPU so
that all the high level logic lives in software. I used only three IP cores from
Gowin:

The \textbf{PicoRV32} soft core is the CPU that runs the firmware and drives every
peripheral over the on chip bus. The \textbf{Gowin rPLL} takes the 27\,MHz board
crystal and produces the two synchronous clocks the design needs (40.5\,MHz for
the bus and the CPU, and a phase shifted copy of it for the SDRAM die).
The \textbf{Gowin FFT} core sits inside the hardware accelerator that I built
around it to turn microphone samples into a spectrum.

Everything else on the FPGA is custom VHDL that I wrote: a generic UART module, a
generic SPI byte engine used to talk to the NOR flash, a second SPI master
specialised and timed for receiving audio from the ADC, a generic GPIO peripheral
that switches the indicator LEDs, and a PWM peripheral that generates the output
tones for the speaker.

On the ESP32 side, the board connects to the FPGA through two independent UART
links, so four wires in total (two TX and two RX), accessed at register level.
The ESP32 runs the AUSP protocol logic: it queues the inbound and outbound
symbols, rebuilds the packets, and decides which packet to send next. The ESP32
is also connected to the internet and exchanges commands with a web dashboard over
a WebSocket. In this arrangement the FPGA is the interface that lets the system
speak my acoustic protocol, and the ESP32 is the brain that runs the protocol on
top of it.

\subsection{The hardware I used}

For persistent storage I used a W25Q64 NOR flash (64\,Mbit, 8\,MByte). The
microphone is an ordinary electret condenser capsule. Its signal is amplified by
an MCP6022 operational amplifier and digitised by an MCP3201 12-bit ADC, which the
FPGA reads over SPI. The speaker is an ordinary 8\,$\Omega$ speaker driven from
the PWM output through a low side MOSFET. Power comes from a single 12\,V supply
that a DFRobot DFR1202 buck module steps down to 3.3\,V for the digital part,
while the 12\,V rail directly feeds the power LED and the speaker stage. Several
RLZ44N MOSFETs do the low side switching, plus the usual capacitors and diodes.

The slave device, the one that receives orders over the air from this master, is
much simpler and is documented elsewhere: it is an ESP32 wired at register level
to an I2S microphone on one side and, through I2S again, to a class D amplifier
and a speaker on the other.

The acoustic link is the only path between master and slave. There is no shared
cable, no Bluetooth, no I2C. Everything the two say to each other, presence
requests, identification, acknowledgements, abort, has to be encoded into tones
and demodulated on the other side (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). The Wi\,Fi link is
only there so I can watch and command the system from a browser; it is not part of
the acoustic protocol.

Inside the master, the ESP32 and the FPGA split the work cleanly. The FPGA does
everything that is timing critical or sample rate bound: it reads the ADC,
computes the FFT, decodes the tones, generates the output PWM tone, and stores the
log on the NOR flash. The ESP32 does everything that is decision bound: it runs
the protocol state machine, talks to the dashboard, and tells the FPGA which tones
to emit. The two are connected by two UARTs, one carrying the decoded and to be
emitted acoustic symbols, the other carrying the flash storage commands.

\subsection{The on chip bus and the address map}

The interconnect (\texttt{wb\_interconnect.vhd}) is a shared bus crossbar with
\textbf{two masters and seven slaves}. The two masters are the PicoRV32 CPU (port
M0) and the audio accelerator (port M1). When both request the bus in the same
cycle, M1 wins: the accelerator is sample rate bound and must not be stalled,
while the CPU can wait a cycle. The slave that answers a transaction is chosen by
decoding the top five address bits \texttt{addr[31:27]}.

Five bits, not four, because of where the soft core keeps its own memory. The IP
treats every address with \texttt{addr[31:29]=000} (\texttt{0x0}--\texttt{0x1FFF\_FFFF})
as \emph{internal} (its ITCM, DTCM and native UART): an external read there never
completes, it just stalls the core. The SDRAM slave therefore lives at
\texttt{0x4800\_0000} (\texttt{addr[31:29]=010}) and not at \texttt{0x1000\_0000};
since that base falls on a half nibble, with bit 27 set, the decoder needs the
fifth bit to tell it apart from the ADC SPI at \texttt{0x4000\_0000}. The NOR flash
is \textbf{not on this bus at all}: it is a self contained hardware block
(\texttt{flash\_ctrl}) that receives storage commands from the ESP32 on its own UART
(pins 72 and 20) and drives the W25Q over its own SPI (pins 42, 41, 51, 48), with no
CPU in the loop, exactly as it worked before the soft core was added.

A transaction works the standard Wishbone way. The active master drives
\texttt{adr}, \texttt{dat}, \texttt{we}, \texttt{sel} and asserts \texttt{cyc} and
\texttt{stb}. The interconnect broadcasts the address and write data to all slaves
but only routes \texttt{stb} and \texttt{cyc} to the one slave selected by
\texttt{addr[31:27]}; the read data and the \texttt{ack} are multiplexed back from
that same slave. A peripheral therefore needs to do nothing more than raise
\texttt{ack} for one cycle when it is addressed. The decode is purely combinational
on five bits, and any address that matches none of the nine windows falls to a sink
that returns zero with no ack.

\subsection{Arbitration}

There are two masters on the bus, so it needs an arbiter. I used static priority,
not round robin: M1 (the audio accelerator) always wins over M0 (the CPU). In the
RTL this is one line, the select signal \texttt{sel\_m1 = m1\_cyc AND m1\_stb}; when
it is high the mux puts M1's \texttt{adr/dat/we/sel/cyc/stb} on the bus, otherwise
M0's. The \texttt{ack} is steered the same way: \texttt{m0\_ack = slv\_ack AND NOT
sel\_m1} and \texttt{m1\_ack = slv\_ack AND sel\_m1}. So while M1 holds the bus, M0
simply receives no \texttt{ack} and stalls on its current access until M1 releases,
which is the standard Wishbone way to make a master wait, with no extra logic.

The reason for the fixed M1 over M0 priority is timing. The accelerator is sample
rate bound: it must move each batch of FFT results out before the next audio window
is ready, and it cannot be made to wait. The CPU has no hard deadline, so it is the
one that yields. The cost to the CPU is small in practice, because M1 only takes the
bus in short bursts: after each audio window the accelerator drains 512 result words
to SDRAM, which is a few tens of microseconds once every 10.9\,ms
(Section~\ref{sec:accel}), so the CPU is blocked for well under 1\,\% of the time and
never for long enough to matter.

\section{Clock tree}
\label{sec:clocks}
% =====================================================================

A single 27\,MHz clock enters the FPGA on pin 4 (\texttt{clk\_i}). The active rPLL
(\texttt{gowin\_rpll.vhd}) is configured \texttt{IDIV\_SEL}$=1$, \texttt{FBDIV\_SEL}$=2$,
so it divides by 2 and multiplies by 3:
\[
f_\mathrm{clkout} = 27\,\mathrm{MHz}\times \frac{3}{2} = 40.5\,\mathrm{MHz}.
\]
The second output \texttt{clkoutp} is the same 40.5\,MHz but phase shifted; with
\texttt{PSDA\_SEL}$=1$ and \texttt{ODIV\_SEL}$=16$ the shift is one of 16 steps,
$360^{\circ}/16 = 22.5^{\circ}$, which at 40.5\,MHz (period 24.7\,ns) is
$24.7\,\mathrm{ns}\times 22.5/360 = 1.54\,\mathrm{ns}$.

There are three clocks, and which block runs on which matters. The 27\,MHz
(\texttt{clk\_i}) is the PLL reference and also directly clocks the PWM speaker, the
PWM LED, the GPIO and the boot banner. The 40.5\,MHz (\texttt{clkout}) is the main
clock: CPU, Wishbone interconnect, SPI for the ADC, audio accelerator, SDRAM
controller, flash SPI, UART and the tone decoder. The 40.5\,MHz phase shifted
(\texttt{clkoutp}) clocks only the SDRAM die, to centre its sampling window
(Section~\ref{sec:sdram}). Table~\ref{tab:dividers} gives the base clock and the
divider each block uses to reach its I/O rate.

\section{SDRAM controller}
\label{sec:sdram}
% =====================================================================

The 8\,MByte die is internal to the GW2AR-18 package and speaks JEDEC SDR SDRAM. It
is not driven directly. A \texttt{memory\_arbiter} sits in front of it and drives the
Gowin SDRC\_HS controller IP. The arbiter has two ports, which is the double input
shown on the SDRAM block in Figure~\ref{fig:soc}: port M0 is the CPU read path
(reached as bus slave S0), port M1 is the audio accelerator write path, wired direct
and not through the main crossbar. M1 has priority over M0, because the accelerator
must drain its FFT results without waiting while the CPU can.

The arbiter is a small FSM around the IP. A write is M1\_CMD (hold \texttt{cmd\_en}
until \texttt{cmd\_ack}) $\to$ M1\_WR\_WAIT (14 cycles to cover WRITE $+ t_\mathrm{WR}
+$ PRECHARGE $+ t_\mathrm{RP}$) $\to$ M1\_ACK. A read is M0\_CMD $\to$ M0\_RD\_WAIT
(5 cycles, CAS latency 3 plus margin, data valid on \texttt{O\_sdrc\_data}) $\to$
M0\_ACK. The controller side uses these die pins: \texttt{O\_sdram\_dq[31:0]} (32 bit
data), \texttt{O\_sdram\_addr[10:0]}, \texttt{O\_sdram\_ba[1:0]} (4 banks),
\texttt{O\_sdram\_dqm[3:0]}, plus CKE / CS / RAS / CAS / WE and the clock. The whole
path runs on \texttt{clk\_sdram} $=40.5$\,MHz; the die is clocked by
\texttt{clkoutp}, the $+1.54$\,ns phase shifted copy.

The Mode Register is programmed with these values:

\begin{itemize}
\item CAS latency = 3 (the read data appears 3 clocks after the READ command);
\item burst length = 1 (I read and write one 16 bit word at a time, because the
      accelerator and the CPU access scattered addresses, not long bursts);
\item burst type = sequential;
\item write burst = single location.
\end{itemize}

I did not use the longer burst lengths or the full page burst mode. I spent a fair
amount of time looking at full page / fast page access, because on paper it gives
the highest throughput, but it only pays off when you stream long contiguous
blocks. My traffic is the opposite: the accelerator writes one FFT bin here, the
CPU reads one word there. With burst length 1 every access is self contained, the
controller stays simple, and I never pay for opening a row I am not going to reuse.
So the design choice was burst length 1 on purpose, not by omission.

\subsection{Bring up sequence}

The order below was found by testing, not by copying a figure. Each step is the
problem I saw and the parameter that fixed it:

\begin{enumerate}
\item READ issued right after power up $\to$ garbage on every access. The die is not
      ready yet. Fix: assert \texttt{CKE}, wait $\ge 200\,\mu$s for the internal
      regulator.
\item write then immediate read back $\to$ still wrong on the first few accesses,
      then correct. The refresh counter was not locked. Fix: two AUTO REFRESH
      commands before programming the Mode Register.
\item PRECHARGE ALL before the refreshes $\to$ needed to put all four banks in the
      idle state first.
\end{enumerate}

So the bring up is: \texttt{CKE} high and wait $\ge 200\,\mu$s; PRECHARGE ALL; two
AUTO REFRESH; program the Mode Register (CL$=3$, BL$=1$, sequential, single write);
after that, ACTIVATE / READ / WRITE / PRECHARGE are allowed.

Two more numbers came from the bench. First, skew: at 40.5\,MHz the clock period is
24.7\,ns, and I was reading \texttt{0xFFFE} for \texttt{0xFFFF} on a small fraction of
accesses, because the latching edge landed at the edge of the data valid window
instead of its centre. Driving the die from \texttt{clkoutp}, the phase shifted copy,
fixed it: with \texttt{PSDA\_SEL}$=1$ and \texttt{ODIV\_SEL}$=16$ the shift is
$360^{\circ}/16 = 22.5^{\circ} = 1.54\,\mathrm{ns}$, enough to move the sampling edge
into the centre of the window, and the bit errors disappeared. Second, refresh: all
8192 rows must be refreshed every 64\,ms, so one AUTO REFRESH every
$64\,\mathrm{ms}/8192 = 7.81\,\mu$s, which is $7.81\,\mu\mathrm{s}\times 40.5\,\mathrm{MHz}
= 316$ cycles. My first controller refreshed too rarely and rows lost charge after a
few seconds; the $7.81\,\mu$s counter made retention solid.

% =====================================================================
\section{Audio accelerator and tone decoder}
\label{sec:accel}
% =====================================================================

The receive path is built from two state machines that run in parallel and a
ping-pong buffer between them. The accelerator turns microphone samples into a
spectrum in SDRAM and raises an IRQ; the CPU starts it once and then, on each IRQ,
reads that spectrum back over the bus (slave S0) and decodes the tones \emph{in
software}, consistent with the design goal of keeping the high level logic on the
soft core. It never touches a raw sample. The capture FSM (\texttt{dma\_proc}) reads the
ADC over SPI and writes samples into \texttt{spi\_raw\_buf}, a 1024 word block RAM
holding two 512 sample windows. The FFT FSM (\texttt{fft\_proc}) takes the window
just filled, feeds it to the Gowin FFT core, collects the 512 spectrum words in
\texttt{fft\_result\_buf}, drains them to SDRAM and raises the IRQ. While one half of
\texttt{spi\_raw\_buf} is being processed, the other half keeps filling, so no
sample is lost.

\subsection{Timing in cycles and in seconds}

Everything is paced by the ADC. One SCK period is the prescaler, 54 bus cycles, so
$54/40.5\,\mathrm{MHz} = 1.333\,\mu$s (SCK $=750$ kHz). One MCP3201 conversion is 16
SCK periods, so

\[
t_\mathrm{sample} = 16\times 54 = 864\ \text{cycles} = \frac{864}{40.5\,\mathrm{MHz}} = 21.33\,\mu\text{s},
\qquad f_s = \frac{1}{21.33\,\mu\text{s}} = 46.875\ \text{kHz}.
\]

One window is 512 samples, so the time to fill it (and the time between IRQs in
steady state) is

\[
t_\mathrm{window} = 16\times 512\times 54 = 442{,}368\ \text{cycles}
= \frac{442{,}368}{40.5\,\mathrm{MHz}} = 10.92\ \text{ms}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{IRQ rate} = 91.6\ \text{Hz}.
\]

The processing of one window must fit inside that 10.92\,ms. Feeding 512 samples to
the FFT, the FFT pipeline, and collecting 512 outputs cost on the order of
$3\times 512 \approx 1500$ cycles at 40.5\,MHz, i.e. about $37\,\mu$s. Draining the
512 result words to SDRAM, at roughly 10 cycles of the 40.5\,MHz clock per word, is
$512\times 10 / 40.5\,\mathrm{MHz} = 126\,\mu$s. So

\[
t_\mathrm{process} \approx 37 + 126 = 163\,\mu\text{s} \;\ll\; t_\mathrm{window} = 10.92\ \text{ms}
\qquad\left(\text{ratio} \approx 67\right).
\]

This is the condition that makes the pipeline work: $t_\mathrm{process} \ll
t_\mathrm{window}$, so processing one window (163\,$\mu$s) finishes long before the
next window is full (10.92\,ms). The ping-pong buffer is what lets the two overlap,
and the margin is large ($67\times$), so a sample is never at risk of being
overwritten before it is read.

\subsection{FFT size and the tone bins}

The FFT is 512 point and runs on samples taken at 46.875\,kHz, so the bin spacing
is

\[
\Delta f = \frac{46875\ \mathrm{Hz}}{512} = 91.6\ \mathrm{Hz}.
\]

A tone at frequency $f$ lands in bin $k = \mathrm{round}(f / \Delta f)$. The five
tones the protocol uses, and the bins the decoder watches, are in
Table~\ref{tab:bins}.

The smallest gap between two used bins is between the EOF at bin 32 and bit 0 at

\subsection{The tone decoder, with its checks}

For each of the five candidate bins, the decoder decides whether that tone is
present. It applies, in order: a local maximum test, the candidate bin magnitude
must be greater than or equal to its six neighbours within $\pm 3$ bins
($k\!-\!3 \dots k\!+\!3$); an amplitude test, the magnitude must exceed a threshold
derived from the running noise floor; and a parabolic interpolation on the three
points $k\!-\!1, k, k\!+\!1$ to confirm the peak is genuinely centred on $k$ and not
the skirt of a neighbour. If all three pass, the decoder emits the character for
that tone on the UART.

The $\pm 3$ neighbourhood, rather than a strict $\pm 1$, is used because the Hann
window applied before the FFT spreads a pure tone over two or three bins.

\section{SPI master for the ADC, and the microphone front end}
\label{sec:adc}
% =====================================================================

The receive chain in front of the FPGA is analog, and getting it described
correctly matters. The electret microphone produces a few millivolts riding on no
particular DC level. An MCP6022 operational amplifier, wired as a non inverting
stage, does two jobs: it biases the signal to mid rail and it amplifies it. The
bias comes from a 47\,k$\Omega$ / 47\,k$\Omega$ divider from the 3.3\,V rail, which
sets the operating point at $3.3/2 = 1.65\,\mathrm{V}$ so that the amplified
waveform swings symmetrically inside the ADC input range. After the amplifier an RC
low pass acts as an anti aliasing filter before the ADC. The MCP3201 is a 12 bit
SPI ADC: it is the SPI slave, and the FPGA SPI master clocks it to read each
conversion. So the chain is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{electret mic} &\rightarrow \text{MCP6022 (gain + 1.65 V bias)} \rightarrow \text{RC anti alias}\\
&\rightarrow \text{MCP3201 (12 bit SPI ADC)} \rightarrow \text{FPGA SPI master}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\subsection{Why a custom SPI master and not a generic one}

The MCP3201 has a fixed and slightly awkward frame: after the chip select goes low
there is a sample/null period, then the 12 data bits come out most significant
first. I wrote a dedicated SPI master for it rather than reuse a generic byte
oriented engine, because here I do not send anything (MOSI is unused), I only need
to clock 16 SCK periods, throw away the leading null bit, and capture exactly 12
bits into a register, then raise a data ready flag the accelerator polls. Tying the
frame to the part lets the sample rate be exact and constant, which the FFT needs.

\section{PWM speaker and LED}
\label{sec:speaker}
% =====================================================================

The transmit side is not a square wave generator, even though that would be the
simplest thing to do. A square wave at frequency $f$ carries all its odd harmonics
($3f, 5f, 7f, \dots$) at large amplitude; on the receiver those harmonics land in
other FFT bins and can be read as tones that were never sent, and they waste output
energy that should sit in the wanted bin. So the block (\texttt{pwm\_generic\_master})
synthesises the tone with direct digital synthesis (DDS) and a sine lookup table,
which keeps almost all of the energy at $f$.

The numbers, all at the 27\,MHz PWM clock. A write to \texttt{0x04} loads two 16 bit
frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$. Each one drives a 24 bit phase accumulator that adds a
phase increment every clock,
\[
  \Delta\phi = \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{f \cdot 2^{24}}{27\,\mathrm{MHz}}\right)
             = (f \cdot K + 2^{23}) \gg 24, \qquad
  K = \mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{2^{48}}{27\,\mathrm{MHz}}\right) = 10{,}424{,}999,
\]
where the right hand form avoids a hardware divide. The top 8 bits of each accumulator
index a 256 entry, 8 bit sine table whose entry
$i$ is $\mathrm{round}(128 + 127 \sin(2\pi i / 256))$, centred at 128. The two sine
samples are added and halved into one 8 bit duty value, so a single pin carries both
tones at once, which is exactly what the protocol needs since it sends superimposed
tone pairs.

That 8 bit duty feeds a free running 8 bit PWM comparator, so the carrier is
$27\,\mathrm{MHz} / 2^8 = 105.5\,\mathrm{kHz}$, far above the audio band. The RC low
pass on the MOSFET gate then recovers the analog two tone sine and rejects the
carrier.

The amplitude is not switched on and off hard either. A hard on/off edge is a step,
which is broadband and would add a click plus spectral splatter into the neighbouring
bins. Instead an attack/release envelope ramps the AC part of the duty (the swing
around the 128 centre) from 0 to full and back over $\mathrm{RAMP\_MS} = 4\,\mathrm{ms}$,
one amplitude step every $\mathrm{RAMP\_TICK} = (27\,\mathrm{MHz}/1000 \cdot 4)/255
\approx 423$ clocks. The phase accumulators keep running through the release, so the
tone fades out instead of cutting. The envelope is the small FSM in
Figure~\ref{fig:pwm-env}.

The duty stream drives the gate of a low side MOSFET (an RLZ44N); the speaker sits
between the 12\,V rail and the MOSFET drain, with a flyback diode across it to absorb
the inductive kick when the MOSFET switches off. There is no operational amplifier in
the speaker path (the op amp belongs to the microphone input chain,
Section~\ref{sec:adc}): the chain is just PWM, RC filter, MOSFET, speaker.

The LED indicator is the 4 bit PWM peripheral, used the same way: its output drives
the gate of a second low side MOSFET that switches a 6\,W power LED on the 12\,V
rail, with a series resistor $R = (V_\mathrm{in}-V_f)/I_\mathrm{LED}$ setting the
current. The 4 bits give 16 brightness levels.

A tone is emitted for a fixed number of cycles, then the transmit path leaves a short
silence before the next tone, so the receiver has time to finish processing one symbol
before the next arrives.

\section{NOR flash controller}
\label{sec:flashctrl}
% =====================================================================

The flash controller is the largest block by line count and the one that caused the
most trouble on the bench. It takes high level requests from the ESP32 over a
dedicated UART (append a 16 byte record, save the 32 byte settings, read a slot,
clear the log) and turns each one into the right sequence of SPI commands to the
external W25Q64 chip.

\subsection{Memory layout}

I use the first 12\,KB of the chip, three 4\,KB sectors (4\,KB is the natural erase
granularity of the part). Sector 0 at \texttt{0x0000} holds the 32 byte settings
(a \texttt{0xA5} magic byte, a 15 byte device name, a 2 byte auto presence interval,
a 1 byte retry count, and padding). Sector 1 at \texttt{0x1000} and sector 2 at
\texttt{0x2000} hold the log, 256 slots of 16 bytes each, so 512 slots together,
managed as a ring buffer. Each 16 byte record is: 2 bytes sequence number, 1 byte
type character (\texttt{B} boot, \texttt{R} registration, \texttt{P} presence,
\texttt{N} note, \texttt{E} event), 4 bytes timestamp, 1 byte value, 8 bytes ASCII
text. Figure~\ref{fig:flash-map} draws this map.

\subsection{SCAN: finding the head after power up}

At power up the controller does not know where the last valid record is. It reads
byte 2 (the type byte) of every one of the 512 slots and treats a slot as valid only
if that byte is one of \texttt{B, R, P, N, E}. Anything else (the \texttt{0xFF} of an
erased slot, or garbage from an aborted write) counts as empty. The head becomes
(highest valid slot $+ 1$) mod 512. This is more robust than stopping at the first
\texttt{0xFF}, which I tried first: that version stopped at any hole, even when valid
records sat past the hole, which is exactly what a failed retry leaves behind.

\subsection{Unlocking the chip and the polling timeout}

Two settings in this block came directly from bench failures, both documented with
their numbers in Section~\ref{sec:si}. At boot the controller issues a write enable,
a WRSR that writes \texttt{0x00} to status register 1 (clearing the block protect
bits BP0..BP2 and the SRP bit) and \texttt{0x02} to status register 2 (setting QE,
which on this part also disables the \texttt{/HOLD\#} and \texttt{/WP\#} pin
functions), then a Global Block Unlock (\texttt{0x98}). The polling timeout that
waits for WIP to clear is set to 25 million bus cycles, about 620\,ms, because a
sector erase on this part can take up to 400\,ms and the first version timed out at
5\,ms and silently abandoned every erase.

\texttt{0x08} & W & CTRL, bit 0 = chip select (1 asserts CS low, held across bytes) \\
\texttt{0x0C} & R & STATUS, bit 0 = busy, bit 1 = done \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Note on the current architecture}

While reworking the SoC I moved the storage SPI onto the bus as a generic, register
driven SPI engine (the \texttt{SPI NOR} block in Figure~\ref{fig:soc}), so that the
W25Q sequence above can be driven by the CPU in firmware rather than by a dedicated
hardware state machine. The sequence itself, the unlock, the probe, the erase
before program, the polling, is unchanged; what changed is who issues it.

% =====================================================================
\section{Signal integrity and decoupling}
\label{sec:si}
% =====================================================================

None of the problems in this section showed up in simulation. All of them showed up
on the bench, and the fixes are a mix of a few capacitors, a slower clock, and
firmware retries. The common thread is that the W25Q64 sits on a breakout board
connected to the FPGA by about 10\,cm of Dupont jumper wires, which is a poor
environment for both power delivery and signal edges.

\subsection{Brownout during sector erase}

To erase a 4\,KB sector the chip runs an internal charge pump that draws around
80\,mA in peaks for about 30\,ms (typical), up to 400\,ms worst case. To program one
record the draw is lighter, around 25\,mA for under 3\,ms. The supply reaches the
chip through the jumper wires, so any current spike shows up as a voltage droop at
the chip.

The droop over a decoupling capacitor follows $\Delta V = I\,t/C$. With no capacitor
at all (only the tens of pF of the wire), the 80\,mA erase current collapsed the
3.3\,V rail almost instantly. The chip browned out mid erase, lost its write enable,
silently rejected the program that followed, and the firmware saw a success that had
not happened.

Adding a 1\,$\mu$F ceramic across VCC and GND at the chip gives

\[
\Delta V = \frac{0.080 \times 0.030}{1\times 10^{-6}} = 2.4\ \mathrm{V},
\]

which still drops the rail to 0.9\,V, below the 2.7\,V minimum, so log writes started
working but settings saves (erase plus program) still failed. Adding a second
capacitor of 10\,$\mu$F in parallel, for 11\,$\mu$F total, gives

\[
\Delta V = \frac{0.080 \times 0.030}{11\times 10^{-6}} = 0.22\ \mathrm{V},
\]

so the rail only drops to 3.08\,V, safely above 2.7\,V, and the brownout is gone. The
two values cover different bands: the 1\,$\mu$F has low ESR/ESL and handles the fast
SPI edges, the 10\,$\mu$F holds up the slow charge pump current. The minimum
capacitance to stay inside a 0.6\,V budget is
$C \ge I\,t/\Delta V = 0.080\times0.030/0.6 = 4\,\mu$F, so 11\,$\mu$F has comfortable
margin.

\subsection{SPI clock too fast for the wiring}

Separately, the first SPI clock of 5\,MHz was too fast for 10\,cm of jumper with a
single ground return. Long writes came out truncated: a 16 byte record would arrive
with byte 10 onward stuck at \texttt{0xFF}. I lowered the clock in steps and watched
the first attempt success rate: 5\,MHz was bad, 2.5\,MHz better, 1.27\,MHz mostly
good, and 633\,kHz (divide by 64) almost always good. At 633\,kHz a bit lasts
1.58\,$\mu$s, long enough that the ringing on the wire settles well before the chip
samples.

\subsection{Retry and verify in firmware}

Even at 633\,kHz an occasional write still fails (roughly 1 in 100 on settings,
rarer on log records). The firmware does up to 5 attempts: send, wait for the
acknowledgement, read the slot back, compare all 16 bytes, retry if they differ. A
failed attempt burns one ring buffer slot, but the SCAN at boot ignores those
because their type byte is not in the valid set, so nothing is lost permanently.
Table~\ref{tab:bench} is the measured reliability after all three fixes.

A separate hardware lesson, worth recording: an indicator LED that I had wired with
its anode straight to an FPGA pin and its cathode to the common ground, with no
series resistor, was injecting tens of mA into the ground return. That current,
through the impedance of the shared ground wire, moved the ground reference enough
to corrupt the microphone ADC readings (the FFT then "heard" tones that were not
there) and to disturb the UART and SPI at the same time. Removing the resistorless
LEDs fixed all of it at once. The rule is to always put a series resistor (330\,$\Omega$
to 1\,k$\Omega$) on an LED driven from a logic pin.

% =====================================================================
\section{ESP32 firmware}
% =====================================================================

The ESP32 firmware is split into small modules. \texttt{uart\_reg} is a register
level driver for the ESP32 UART1 and UART2 peripherals, with no Arduino
HardwareSerial layer. \texttt{flash\_link} performs the high level flash operations
against the FPGA controller, with retry and verify and a 60 record RAM cache for the
dashboard. \texttt{fpga\_uart\_link} is the codebook layer that turns decoded
characters into protocol patterns and drives the speaker through the FPGA.
\texttt{protocol} is the acoustic protocol state machine (presence, retries, abort,
registration). \texttt{web\_link} is the WebSocket client and JSON layer for the
dashboard.

\subsection{Register level UART}

The two UART links to the FPGA are driven by writing the UART peripheral registers
directly (the FIFO, CLKDIV, CONF0 and STATUS registers of UART1 and UART2), not
through HardwareSerial. The baud divider for 115200 from the 80\,MHz APB clock is
\[
\mathrm{int} = \left\lfloor \frac{80\,000\,000}{115200}\right\rfloor = 694,\qquad
\mathrm{frac} = \left\lfloor \frac{80\,000\,000\times 16}{115200}\right\rfloor \bmod 16 = 7,
\]
so the CLKDIV register is $694\,|\,(7\ll 20)=\texttt{0x7002B6}$, giving
$80\,000\,000/(694+7/16)=115{,}208$ baud, $0.007\%$ off. A free running 1\,MHz
hardware timer (TIMG0) gives microsecond timeouts for the UART reads without using
\texttt{micros()}.

\subsection{Flash link: retry and verify, and a defensive read}

When the dashboard sends a note, \texttt{web\_link} parses the JSON and calls
\texttt{flash\_log\_append}, which builds the 16 byte record, sends opcode
\texttt{L} plus the bytes over UART1, waits for the \texttt{K} acknowledgement,
reads the head back with \texttt{H}, reads the just written slot back with
\texttt{G}, and compares all 16 bytes; on a mismatch it retries, up to 5 times. The
record is also pushed into the RAM cache so the dashboard still shows it even if a
later NOR read misbehaves. The settings save works the same way against the 32 byte
sector. The settings read, \texttt{flash\_get\_settings}, validates before
returning: the magic byte must be \texttt{0xA5}, the retry count must be between 1
and 30, and the name must be printable ASCII; if any check fails it returns safe
defaults, so the dashboard never shows a garbled name or an absurd count even when
the NOR has a half written sector.

% =====================================================================
\section{The acoustic protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
% =====================================================================

The protocol uses an alternating bit codebook. A pattern is two or three symbols,
with the rule that two consecutive symbols must differ. This makes detection robust,
because the decoder triggers on a \emph{change} of tone, which is easy to see, rather
than on silence, which is hard to measure over a noisy channel. There are six
symbols: lowercase \texttt{a}, \texttt{b}, \texttt{c} on the 2000\,Hz master carrier
(bit 0, bit 1, end of frame), and uppercase \texttt{A}, \texttt{B}, \texttt{C} on the
2400\,Hz slave carrier. The master sends lowercase and the slave sends uppercase, so
each side knows who spoke. A few patterns from master to slave: \texttt{ab} is a
presence request, \texttt{ba} assigns an id, \texttt{aba} is an abort.

The channel is half duplex, so only one side transmits at a time. The master does
carrier sense: it watches the decoder for any uppercase symbol and waits for the
channel to be quiet for at least one second before transmitting. Retries are handled
by the ESP32: a presence request with no answer within five seconds is repeated up
to a configurable number of times (stored in the flash settings), after which the
master gives up and tells the dashboard.

% =====================================================================
\section{Power supply}
% =====================================================================

Power enters as a single 12\,V supply rated 2\,A. A fuse and a series Schottky diode
protect against overcurrent and reverse polarity, then an LM2596 based DFRobot
DFR1202 buck module steps the 12\,V down to 3.3\,V at up to 0.5\,A for all the
digital parts (the FPGA board, the ESP32, the NOR flash, the ADC). Input and output
of the buck are decoupled with 100\,$\mu$F and 100\,nF capacitors. The 12\,V rail is
kept separate from the buck and feeds the two high current loads directly: the 6\,W
power LED and the speaker stage, each switched by its own low side MOSFET from a
logic pin. Keeping the LED and speaker on the raw 12\,V rather than on the 3.3\,V
buck keeps their switching current off the supply that feeds the sensitive ADC and
flash.

% =====================================================================
\section{What worked and what did not}
% =====================================================================

The parts that worked end to end are the bus and its address map, the SDRAM
controller once the refresh interval and the 1.54\,ns clock phase shift were set, the
512 point FFT with the parabolic peak decoder and its $\pm 3$ neighbourhood, the
alternating bit codebook (helped a lot by giving each direction its own short
sub codebook for the most repeated message, the presence request), the NOR flash
controller after the decoupling and clock work, the WebSocket dashboard, and the
register level UART driver on the ESP32.

A few things were tried and dropped. The early design had a generic DMA between the
ADC and SDRAM with the CPU programming descriptors; it spent too many CPU cycles per
window, so I folded the gather and FFT launch into the streaming accelerator. The
$\pm 1$ decoder neighbourhood was too strict and was widened to $\pm 3$. The plain
HardwareSerial on the ESP32 worked but did not show register level work, so I
replaced it with the direct register driver.

What still wobbles is mostly the wiring. The W25Q breakout is on Dupont jumpers, so
the SPI clock cannot realistically go above 633\,kHz; on a proper board with a
ground plane it would run far faster with no retries. The decoupling is 1 plus
10\,$\mu$F rather than the more orthodox 100\,nF plus 10\,$\mu$F, because that is
what I had on hand. And the very first status register read after power up sometimes
returns \texttt{0xFF} before the chip is ready, which makes the firmware print a
false "chip locked" warning that clears on the next read.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
% =====================================================================

The master node is a working integration of a custom SoC on the Gowin GW2AR-18, an
acoustic receive pipeline from analog microphone through a 12 bit ADC, a 512 point
FFT and a tone decoder, a NOR flash store driven over SPI, and an ESP32 that runs my
AUSP protocol on top and bridges it to a web dashboard. The single most useful
lesson was that for the mixed signal parts the datasheet number is where the problem
starts, not where it ends: the simple relation $\Delta V = I\,t/C$ explained a
"flash write succeeds but the data is not there" bug that had looked like a firmware
fault for days.
